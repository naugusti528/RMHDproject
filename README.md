# This is a series of projects detailing my progress of simulations of general relativistic magnetohydrodynamics (GRMHD).

## Project no.1: The Brio Wu shock tube

### Non technical explanation:
Physics, regardless of the specialized field, starts with using your imagination. Imagine two sealed rooms with air, where one is super hot with a lot of pressure, and another is really cold with very low pressure, with a thin wall separating them. Let's say that wall suddenly vanishes; you'd think those two rooms (now one big room) would calmly balance out, but the air actually violently reorganizes itself. There's a shock wave compressing air into the low-pressure area and a rarefaction wave spreading air out of the high-pressure area. This is the classic "shock tube" experiment, and it's exactly the setup (mathematically) that the Brio-Wu test uses. Instead of air, the fluid is plasma, which is matter so hot that electrons strip away from their nuclei, leaving a charged soup that's got pressure, temperature, and a lot of electric static. While we don't see plasma in our lives, it's the dominant state of matter in stars, accretion disks, and astrophysical jets, regions that the Brio Wu shock tube somewehat predicts the behavior of.

The Brio Wu shock tube is a standard benchmark for testing whether a relativistic MHD code reproduces this known wave structure correctly. I'm running this in special relativistic MHD (SRMHD - no gravity, just regular stuff moving really fast) as a step toward the general relativistic case, where I'll then deal with plasma under severe gravity. My focus is on where the numerics of the solver break down: the math equations that tell us what's related will always be the same (i.e. E will always equal mc^2), but the algorithm that recovers physical quantities (density, pressure, velocity) can fail to converge, or return nonsense results, in extreme environments. For now, I'm trying to see how exactly each physical quantity breaks the server, at what values, and why.
